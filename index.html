🔴 Upper Motor Neuron Signs

  • Spasticity (velocity-dependent increased tone)
  • Hyperreflexia (exaggerated DTRs)
  • Babinski sign (upgoing plantar reflex)
  • Pseudobulbar affect (emotional lability)
  • Clonus · Hoffman's sign
  • Slow effortful voluntary movement

🟢 Lower Motor Neuron Signs

  • Muscle atrophy (amyotrophy)
  • Fasciculations (visible muscle twitching)
  • Hyporeflexia (reduced or absent DTRs)
  • Hypotonia (decreased muscle tone)
  • Weakness in myotomal distribution
  • Cramps

Deep Dive

Excitotoxicity & Glial Toxicity

The two best-understood and therapeutically tractable pathways in ALS.

⚡ The Excitotoxicity Cascade

  • EAAT2 (GLT-1) expression falls 60–70% in ALS motor cortex and spinal cord
  • Synaptic glutamate accumulates → persistent AMPA/NMDA activation
  • Sustained Ca²⁺ influx activates calpains, caspases, and nitric oxide synthase
  • Low GluR2 expression in motor neurons = high Ca²⁺ permeability through AMPA receptors
  • Mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload → cytochrome c → intrinsic apoptosis

💊 Riluzole — FDA 1995

  • Inhibits Na⁺ channels on pre-synaptic terminals → reduces glutamate release
  • Blocks NMDA receptor activation at clinical doses
  • Extends median survival ~2–3 months in two pivotal RCTs
  • Now available as oral film (Exservan), liquid (Tiglutik) — easier for dysphagic patients
  • Mechanism: anti-excitotoxic rather than neuroprotective per se

Rothstein JD et al. (1995) N Engl J Med 326:1464 · Bensimon G et al. (1994) N Engl J Med 330:585

🔥 Toxic Astrocytes

  • SOD1-mutant astrocytes release soluble factors that selectively kill motor neurons in co-culture
  • C9orf72 astrocytes cause progressive loss of action potential output in motor neurons
  • Mechanisms: excess NO, glutamate, TRAIL, FasL, extracellular vesicles
  • EAAT2 downregulation in reactive astrocytes amplifies excitotoxicity

🔥 Microglia & Immune Cells

  • Activated microglia shift to M1 phenotype: TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, NO release
  • NK cells infiltrate motor cortex and spinal cord; kill motor neurons via NKG2D receptor
  • Complement C1q and C5 contribute to denervation and macrophage recruitment
  • Mast cells + neutrophils infiltrate skeletal muscle NMJ; neutrophils phagocytose degenerating axons

Nagai M et al. (2007) Nat Neurosci 10:615 · Shi Y et al. (2018) Nat Neurosci 21:423

1

SOD1 Mutation (A4V, G93A, D90A…)

Normal SOD1 converts O₂⁻ to H₂O₂. Mutant SOD1 misfolds, disrupts mitochondrial outer membrane integrity, and generates aberrant peroxidase activity producing hydroxyl radicals (·OH). Toxic gain-of-function — not loss of dismutase activity.

2

ROS Accumulation & Mitochondrial Damage

Excess ROS damage mitochondrial DNA, lipid membranes, and ETC complexes I and IV. This disrupts ΔΨm and ATP synthesis — creating an energy crisis in the metabolically demanding motor neuron (~100× more ATP than average neurons).

3

NAD⁺ Depletion — Gut Microbiome Link

Nicotinamide (NAD precursor) from gut microbiota (Akkermansia muciniphila) is depleted in ALS. Supplementation improves mitochondrial function and reduces oxidative stress gene expression in SOD1 mice — a novel disease-modifying avenue.

4

mPTP Opening → Apoptosis

Combined Ca²⁺ overload (excitotoxicity) + ROS opens the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, releasing cytochrome c and triggering caspase-9 → caspase-3 intrinsic apoptosis. AMX0035 targets this step via taurursodiol (TUDCA).

Rosen DR et al. (1993) Nature 362:59 · Blacher E et al. (2019) Nature 572:474 (gut microbiome ALS)

Genetic Architecture

The genetic landscape of ALS — from the most common repeat expansion (C9orf72) to rare Mendelian mutations, each with distinct pathogenic mechanisms.

The Four Major Genes

C9orf72 · SOD1 · TARDBP · FUS

Together these genes account for ~65–70% of familial ALS (~10% of total ALS). Each drives a distinct pathogenic pathway and has unique therapeutic implications.

C9
C9orf72
Chr 9p21.2 · Non-coding repeat expansion
~40%
of familial ALS · Most common genetic cause worldwide
GGGGCC hexanucleotide repeat expansion in intron 1. Normal <30 repeats; ALS = hundreds to thousands. Three simultaneous mechanisms: (1) loss of C9orf72 function (autophagy/lysosome), (2) toxic RNA foci sequestering hnRNP proteins and nucleolin, (3) RAN translation producing five dipeptide repeat proteins (poly-GR, poly-PR most toxic → disrupt NCT and ribosomes).
🔬 Poly-GR and poly-PR DPRs cause phase separation defects and nucleocytoplasmic transport failure — downstream TDP-43 mislocalization
SOD1
SOD1
Chr 21q22.11 · Enzyme · First ALS gene (1993)
~20%
of familial ALS · >180 pathogenic mutations
Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase 1. A4V = most aggressive in North America (survival <1 yr from onset). G93A = standard mouse model. Toxic gain-of-function: misfolded SOD1 disrupts mitochondrial outer membrane, generates aberrant peroxidase activity, creates ROS. Aggregates immunoreactive with C4F6 antibody. TDP-43 pathology absent.
💊 Tofersen (ASO) degrades SOD1 mRNA via RNase H — FDA approved April 2023; >60% reduction in SOD1 protein and plasma NfL

TARDBP
Chr 1p36.2 · RNA-binding protein
~5%
of familial ALS · Pathology in >97% of ALL ALS
Encodes TDP-43 (TAR DNA-binding protein 43). Mutations in glycine-rich C-terminal domain (M337V, G298S, A315T, Q331K). Even without TARDBP mutation, TDP-43 nuclear clearance + cytoplasmic aggregation = hallmark of >97% of all ALS. Downstream targets: STMN2 (axonal repair), UNC13A (synaptic docking), CFTR.
🧬 STMN2 cryptic exon splicing caused by TDP-43 loss is among the most druggable downstream consequences
FUS
FUS/TLS
Chr 16p11.2 · RNA-binding protein
~4%
of familial ALS · Major cause of juvenile ALS
Fused in Sarcoma (FUS/TLS). Mutations in C-terminal nuclear localisation signal (R521C/G/H; P525L most severe). Cytoplasmic mislocalization → FUS-positive, TDP-43-negative basophilic inclusions. P525L causes teenage onset with aggressive progression. De novo FUS mutations account for ~1% of apparently sporadic ALS — screen young-onset patients.
⚠️ De novo FUS mutations: test all patients with ALS onset <40 years — even without family history

Imaging & Diagnosis

MRI signatures of upper motor neuron degeneration, fluid biomarkers tracking axonal injury, and the Gold Coast diagnostic criteria.

Neuroimaging

MRI Signatures of ALS

Three MRI patterns support UMN degeneration. Their absence does not exclude ALS — but presence provides strong corroboration.

🔵 Motor Band Sign

  • Hypointense band on SWI along precentral gyrus
  • Iron accumulation from microglial activation
  • Sensitivity ~56%, specificity ~75%
  • More prominent in early UMN-dominant ALS

🟣 CST T2 Hyperintensity

  • Bilateral T2/FLAIR signal ↑ along corticospinal tract
  • From IC → brainstem → spinal cord
  • Wallerian-like degeneration of UMN axons
  • Best seen on 3T MRI with STIR or FLAIR

🔴 Anterior Horn Atrophy

  • Loss of anterior horn grey matter volume
  • Correlates with LMN burden and severity
  • Quantified by spinal cord morphometry
  • Longitudinal biomarker for clinical trials

Cheong I et al. (2022) JNNP · Kolind S et al. (2021) Brain · van der Burgh HK et al. (2020) JNNP

Diagnostic Criteria

Gold Coast Criteria (2020) — Current Standard

The Gold Coast Criteria replaced El Escorial as the primary diagnostic framework — more inclusive, better reflecting clinical reality.

📋 Core Requirements▼

All three of the following are required:

  • Progressive motor impairment documented by history or examination
  • UMN AND LMN dysfunction in at least one body region — OR — LMN in ≥2 regions
  • Investigations excluding alternative diagnoses (MRI, EMG, labs, genetics)
⚡ EMG Criteria (LMN dysfunction)▼

EMG findings confirming LMN degeneration:

  • Active denervation: fibrillation potentials and/or positive sharp waves in ≥2 muscles
  • Chronic neurogenic changes: large polyphasic long-duration MUPs
  • Fasciculation potentials: spontaneous motor unit firing
  • Must be documented in ≥2 of 4 regions: bulbar, cervical, thoracic, lumbosacral
🧠 UMN Signs on Examination▼

Upper motor neuron involvement must be confirmed clinically:

  • Spasticity — velocity-dependent increased tone
  • Hyperreflexia — exaggerated deep tendon reflexes
  • Pathological reflexes — Babinski sign, Hoffmann's sign
  • Jaw jerk · Pseudobulbar affect · Slow voluntary movement
🚫 Exclusion Requirements▼

Must rule out before confirming ALS:

  • Structural causes: cervical spondylotic myelopathy, foramen magnum lesion (MRI)
  • Multifocal motor neuropathy: anti-GM1 antibodies, conduction block on NCS
  • Kennedy disease (SBMA): AR CAG repeat; only LMN + sensory; androgen insensitivity
  • Metabolic: hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, B12/copper deficiency
  • Hereditary spastic paraplegia: pure UMN; genetic panel

Shefner JM et al. (2020) Amyotroph Lateral Scler Frontotemporal Degener 21:1–12
