Clinical Subtypes

🔴 Upper Motor Neuron Signs

  • Spasticity (velocity-dependent increased tone)
  • Hyperreflexia (exaggerated DTRs)
  • Babinski sign (upgoing plantar reflex)
  • Pseudobulbar affect (emotional lability)
  • Clonus · Hoffman's sign
  • Slow effortful voluntary movement

🟢 Lower Motor Neuron Signs

  • Muscle atrophy (amyotrophy)
  • Fasciculations (visible muscle twitching)
  • Hyporeflexia (reduced or absent DTRs)
  • Hypotonia (decreased muscle tone)
  • Weakness in myotomal distribution
  • Cramps

Molecular Mechanisms

The core pathogenic cascades driving motor neuron death — excitotoxicity, protein aggregation, oxidative stress, and glial toxicity.

Master Cascade

ALS Pathogenic Cascade — Overview

Multiple converging mechanisms ultimately drive motor neuron apoptosis. Each step is a potential therapeutic target.

Deep Dive

Excitotoxicity & Glial Toxicity

The two best-understood and therapeutically tractable pathways in ALS.

⚡ The Excitotoxicity Cascade

  • EAAT2 (GLT-1) expression falls 60–70% in ALS motor cortex and spinal cord
  • Synaptic glutamate accumulates → persistent AMPA/NMDA activation
  • Sustained Ca²⁺ influx activates calpains, caspases, and nitric oxide synthase
  • Low GluR2 expression in motor neurons = high Ca²⁺ permeability through AMPA receptors
  • Mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload → cytochrome c → intrinsic apoptosis

💊 Riluzole — FDA 1995

  • Inhibits Na⁺ channels on pre-synaptic terminals → reduces glutamate release
  • Blocks NMDA receptor activation at clinical doses
  • Extends median survival ~2–3 months in two pivotal RCTs
  • Now available as oral film (Exservan), liquid (Tiglutik) — easier for dysphagic patients
  • Mechanism: anti-excitotoxic rather than neuroprotective per se

Rothstein JD et al. (1995) N Engl J Med 326:1464 · Bensimon G et al. (1994) N Engl J Med 330:585

🔥 Toxic Astrocytes

  • SOD1-mutant astrocytes release soluble factors that selectively kill motor neurons in co-culture
  • C9orf72 astrocytes cause progressive loss of action potential output in motor neurons
  • Mechanisms: excess NO, glutamate, TRAIL, FasL, extracellular vesicles
  • EAAT2 downregulation in reactive astrocytes amplifies excitotoxicity

🔥 Microglia & Immune Cells

  • Activated microglia shift to M1 phenotype: TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, NO release
  • NK cells infiltrate motor cortex and spinal cord; kill motor neurons via NKG2D receptor
  • Complement C1q and C5 contribute to denervation and macrophage recruitment
  • Mast cells + neutrophils infiltrate skeletal muscle NMJ; neutrophils phagocytose degenerating axons

Nagai M et al. (2007) Nat Neurosci 10:615 · Shi Y et al. (2018) Nat Neurosci 21:423

1

SOD1 Mutation (A4V, G93A, D90A…)

Normal SOD1 converts O₂⁻ to H₂O₂. Mutant SOD1 misfolds, disrupts mitochondrial outer membrane integrity, and generates aberrant peroxidase activity producing hydroxyl radicals (·OH). Toxic gain-of-function — not loss of dismutase activity.

2

ROS Accumulation & Mitochondrial Damage

Excess ROS damage mitochondrial DNA, lipid membranes, and ETC complexes I and IV. This disrupts ΔΨm and ATP synthesis — creating an energy crisis in the metabolically demanding motor neuron (~100× more ATP than average neurons).

3

NAD⁺ Depletion — Gut Microbiome Link

Nicotinamide (NAD precursor) from gut microbiota (Akkermansia muciniphila) is depleted in ALS. Supplementation improves mitochondrial function and reduces oxidative stress gene expression in SOD1 mice — a novel disease-modifying avenue.

4

mPTP Opening → Apoptosis

Combined Ca²⁺ overload (excitotoxicity) + ROS opens the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, releasing cytochrome c and triggering caspase-9 → caspase-3 intrinsic apoptosis. AMX0035 targets this step via taurursodiol (TUDCA).

Rosen DR et al. (1993) Nature 362:59 · Blacher E et al. (2019) Nature 572:474 (gut microbiome ALS)

Genetic Architecture

The genetic landscape of ALS — from the most common repeat expansion (C9orf72) to rare Mendelian mutations, each with distinct pathogenic mechanisms.

The Four Major Genes

C9orf72 · SOD1 · TARDBP · FUS

Together these genes account for ~65–70% of familial ALS (~10% of total ALS). Each drives a distinct pathogenic pathway and has unique therapeutic implications.

Imaging & Diagnosis

MRI signatures of upper motor neuron degeneration, fluid biomarkers tracking axonal injury, and the Gold Coast diagnostic criteria.

Neuroimaging

MRI Signatures of ALS

Three MRI patterns support UMN degeneration. Their absence does not exclude ALS — but presence provides strong corroboration.

🔵 Motor Band Sign

  • Hypointense band on SWI along precentral gyrus
  • Iron accumulation from microglial activation
  • Sensitivity ~56%, specificity ~75%
  • More prominent in early UMN-dominant ALS

🟣 CST T2 Hyperintensity

  • Bilateral T2/FLAIR signal ↑ along corticospinal tract
  • From IC → brainstem → spinal cord
  • Wallerian-like degeneration of UMN axons
  • Best seen on 3T MRI with STIR or FLAIR

🔴 Anterior Horn Atrophy

  • Loss of anterior horn grey matter volume
  • Correlates with LMN burden and severity
  • Quantified by spinal cord morphometry
  • Longitudinal biomarker for clinical trials

Cheong I et al. (2022) JNNP · Kolind S et al. (2021) Brain · van der Burgh HK et al. (2020) JNNP

Diagnostic Criteria

Gold Coast Criteria (2020) — Current Standard

The Gold Coast Criteria replaced El Escorial as the primary diagnostic framework — more inclusive, better reflecting clinical reality.

📋 Core Requirements▼

All three of the following are required:

  • Progressive motor impairment documented by history or examination
  • UMN AND LMN dysfunction in at least one body region — OR — LMN in ≥2 regions
  • Investigations excluding alternative diagnoses (MRI, EMG, labs, genetics)
⚡ EMG Criteria (LMN dysfunction)▼

EMG findings confirming LMN degeneration:

  • Active denervation: fibrillation potentials and/or positive sharp waves in ≥2 muscles
  • Chronic neurogenic changes: large polyphasic long-duration MUPs
  • Fasciculation potentials: spontaneous motor unit firing
  • Must be documented in ≥2 of 4 regions: bulbar, cervical, thoracic, lumbosacral
🧠 UMN Signs on Examination▼

Upper motor neuron involvement must be confirmed clinically:

  • Spasticity — velocity-dependent increased tone
  • Hyperreflexia — exaggerated deep tendon reflexes
  • Pathological reflexes — Babinski sign, Hoffmann's sign
  • Jaw jerk · Pseudobulbar affect · Slow voluntary movement
🚫 Exclusion Requirements▼

Must rule out before confirming ALS:

  • Structural causes: cervical spondylotic myelopathy, foramen magnum lesion (MRI)
  • Multifocal motor neuropathy: anti-GM1 antibodies, conduction block on NCS
  • Kennedy disease (SBMA): AR CAG repeat; only LMN + sensory; androgen insensitivity
  • Metabolic: hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, B12/copper deficiency
  • Hereditary spastic paraplegia: pure UMN; genetic panel

Shefner JM et al. (2020) Amyotroph Lateral Scler Frontotemporal Degener 21:1–12

0 of 25 revealed
01 / 25Myth

ALS is a disease of old people.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
01 / 25Fact

Most people are diagnosed in their 50s and 60s, but ALS can start in the 20s, 30s and 40s. Young-onset ALS (under 45) is about 10% of cases.

Sources: ALS Association: Five Myths · Answer ALS: ALS Myths · NINDS: ALS
02 / 25Myth

ALS always runs in families.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
02 / 25Fact

About 90% of cases are sporadic, with no family history. About 10% are familial, caused by changes in specific genes.

Sources: ALS Association: Five Myths · NINDS: ALS
03 / 25Myth

ALS only hits men, or only one ethnic group.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
03 / 25Fact

Men are slightly more likely overall, but at older ages men and women are equally likely to be diagnosed. ALS affects all races and ethnicities, though it is diagnosed most often in non-Hispanic White people.

Sources: NINDS: ALS
04 / 25Myth

ALS is caused by an infection like Lyme disease.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
04 / 25Fact

Research has found no evidence that Lyme disease or other infections cause ALS. Lyme can be mistaken for ALS because symptoms overlap, which is one reason a specialist work-up matters.

Sources: Answer ALS: ALS Myths
05 / 25Myth

ALS never affects thinking or behaviour.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
05 / 25Fact

Many people keep normal thinking, but changes are common. Up to about half show some cognitive difficulty, and roughly 10 to 20% develop frontotemporal dementia. Sudden crying or laughing (pseudobulbar affect) can also occur on its own.

Sources: ALS Association: Five Myths · Answer ALS: ALS Myths · Cognitive dysfunction review (PMC)
06 / 25Myth

Paralysis means people with ALS can't feel pain or touch.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
06 / 25Fact

Even at an advanced stage, people with ALS can still see, hear, smell and feel touch. The nerves that carry pain, heat, cold and pressure are not affected.

Sources: ALS Association: Five Myths
07 / 25Myth

ALS shuts down every muscle, including the heart, bladder and bowel.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
07 / 25Fact

ALS affects voluntary muscles. Involuntary muscles, such as those controlling the heart, gut, bowel, bladder and sexual function, are not directly affected.

Sources: ALS Association: Five Myths
08 / 25Myth

ALS looks the same in everyone.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
08 / 25Fact

Onset, speed and order of symptoms differ a lot. Some people progress slowly, others quickly. One person may lose swallowing early while still walking and driving.

Sources: ALS Association: Five Myths
09 / 25Myth

Everyone with ALS dies within two to five years.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
09 / 25Fact

Most people with ALS die within three to five years of first symptoms, usually from respiratory failure, but about 1 in 10 live 10 years or more. More than half live over three years after diagnosis.

Sources: NINDS: ALS · ALS Association: Five Myths
10 / 25Myth

Stephen Hawking's 55 years with ALS shows what's typical.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
10 / 25Fact

Hawking was diagnosed at 21 and lived 55 years with ALS. That was an unusually slow course, helped by exceptional care. About 1 in 10 people live 10 years or more, but decades is rare.

Sources: NBC News 2018 · MND Victoria · NINDS: ALS
11 / 25Myth

A head injury or contact sport causes ALS.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
11 / 25Fact

Head injury and trauma are not proven causes of ALS. Some studies link head injury to higher risk, but more research is needed to understand it.

Sources: Answer ALS: ALS Myths · NINDS: ALS
12 / 25Myth

Military service has nothing to do with ALS risk.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
12 / 25Fact

Studies suggest US veterans are about 1.5 to 2 times more likely to develop ALS, and the reason is unclear. The VA treats ALS as a presumed service-connected condition.

Sources: US VA: ALS System of Care · NINDS: ALS
13 / 25Myth

There is no cure because nobody is trying to find one.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
13 / 25Fact

Decades of failed trials came from a limited understanding of the biology, not a lack of interest. Research is now speeding up, with growing industry investment and open data sharing.

Sources: Answer ALS: ALS Myths · NINDS: ALS
14 / 25Myth

The Ice Bucket Challenge money was wasted.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
14 / 25Fact

The challenge raised about $115 million for ALS research in the US in two months. It funded Project MinE work that identified the NEK1 gene as linked to ALS in 2016.

Sources: BBC News 2016 · ALS Association: NEK1
15 / 25Myth

There are no treatments for ALS.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
15 / 25Fact

There is no cure, but riluzole, edaravone and tofersen (for SOD1 ALS) can slow decline or extend survival. Symptom treatment and specialist care also improve quality of life.

Sources: NINDS: ALS
16 / 25Myth

Riluzole or edaravone cures or reverses ALS.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
16 / 25Fact

Riluzole may extend survival by a few months. Edaravone has been shown to slow functional decline in some people. Neither reverses damage to motor neurons.

Sources: NINDS: ALS
17 / 25Myth

Tofersen is for everyone with ALS.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
17 / 25Fact

Tofersen is given by spinal injection only to people with a confirmed mutation in the SOD1 gene.

Sources: NINDS: ALS
18 / 25Myth

Stem cell clinics offer a proven cure.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
18 / 25Fact

No stem cell product is approved to cure ALS. The FDA warns about unapproved regenerative products, and after the NurOwn phase 3 trial it said the data did not support clinical benefit.

Sources: FDA: Unapproved regenerative products · FDA: NurOwn update
19 / 25Myth

Nothing can ease ALS symptoms, so it's just waiting.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
19 / 25Fact

Medicines can help with cramps, stiffness, saliva, pain, sleep and episodes of crying or laughing. Physical therapy, speech therapy, feeding support and non-invasive ventilation also help.

Sources: NINDS: ALS · NICE NG42
20 / 25Myth

Specialist ALS clinics make no real difference.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
20 / 25Fact

Specialised multidisciplinary ALS care has been shown to extend survival and improve quality of life. NICE recommends a clinic-based specialist team approach.

Sources: Multidisciplinary care review (PMC) · NICE NG42
21 / 25Myth

People with ALS should avoid all exercise.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
21 / 25Fact

Low-impact exercise such as walking, swimming or a stationary bike, plus range-of-motion work, can help maintain strength and function. The programme should fit the person's ability and fatigue, set by a therapist.

Sources: NINDS: ALS · NICE NG42
22 / 25Myth

Once speech is gone, communication is over.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
22 / 25Fact

Speech therapists can set up alphabet boards, speech-generating devices, eye-tracking systems and banked personal voices. People can still use the internet and type on custom screens.

Sources: NINDS: ALS · NICE NG42
23 / 25Myth

Muscle twitching means you have ALS.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
23 / 25Fact

Twitching on its own is usually harmless (benign fasciculation). In ALS, twitching comes with progressive weakness and muscle wasting, and often appears in several muscles at once. See a doctor if weakness develops.

Sources: Cleveland Clinic: Benign fasciculation
24 / 25Myth

A single blood test can diagnose ALS.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
24 / 25Fact

There is no single test that can definitely diagnose ALS. Neurologists use history, examination over time, EMG and MRI, plus blood, urine and spinal fluid tests to rule out other conditions.

Sources: NINDS: ALS

ALS is a type of multiple sclerosis.

Tap to reveal the fact ↻
25 / 25Fact

They are different diseases. MS is an autoimmune disease that damages myelin. ALS causes motor neurons to die.

Practical guide

Everyday situations, step by step

Filter by area, or search. Tap a card to open it. Each card has steps for the person with ALS, steps for caregivers, when to get help, and its sources.

If you live with ALS

  • Take smaller bites and sips, and swallow two or three times per mouthful.
  • Try the head position a speech therapist recommends. Chin down helps some people, head back helps others.
  • Moisten dry food with sauce or gravy, and sip between bites.
  • Avoid mixed textures like cereal in milk or chunky soup. Thicker drinks are easier to control, and thickener can be added.

If you are a caregiver

  • Help them sit upright with no distractions while eating.
  • If their cough is weak, ask the team to teach you manual assisted cough before you need it.
  • Keep a suction machine close if one was prescribed.
When to get help If food or fluid blocks the airway and the person cannot breathe, cough or speak, call your local emergency number straight away. After any choking episode, ask for a swallowing assessment.
Sources: ALS Association: Speech and swallowing · NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • Tell the team early. They should check weight, diet, fluids and swallowing at each visit.
  • Switch to single-texture foods such as oatmeal, thick cream soups, purées or pudding if chewing gets hard.
  • Ask about a feeding tube (gastrostomy) early. The team should raise it at regular intervals, and some people choose not to have one.

If you are a caregiver

  • Ask the team about eating aids, altered utensils and help with food preparation.
  • Check seating and posture at the table. Low mood can also reduce appetite, so mention it.
When to get help Weight loss, falling intake or fear of choking is a reason to ask for an earlier team review rather than waiting for the next appointment.
Sources: NICE NG42 · ALS Association: Speech and swallowing

If you live with ALS

  • Saliva usually pools because swallowing is weak, not because there is more of it.
  • Early on, wipe it away or swallow on purpose. Very sweet or sour foods can increase it.
  • Ask the doctor about medicines to reduce saliva. NICE lists antimuscarinic medicines and botulinum toxin as options.
  • For thick saliva, ask about fluids, humidification, nebulisers and a review of current medicines.

If you are a caregiver

  • Keep a portable suction machine charged and clean.
  • Help with mouth care and sitting upright.
When to get help Pooled saliva can cause choking, especially at mealtimes. Tell the team if it gets worse.
Sources: ALS Association: Excessive saliva · NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • Choose dishes with a single texture and ask the kitchen to cut or moisten food.
  • Bring your own thickener if you use one.
  • Give eating your full attention, take small bites, and leave extra time.
  • Talk to the speech therapist about what is safe in restaurants.

If you are a caregiver

  • Sit beside them and keep conversation relaxed.
  • Let them eat without help when they can. The team should consider wanting to eat independently in social settings.
When to get help Choking that does not clear, or repeated coughing at meals, needs a call to the team.
Sources: NICE NG42 · ALS Association: Speech and swallowing

If you live with ALS

  • These can be early signs of weak breathing muscles: breathlessness, trouble breathing lying flat, recurrent chest infections, a weak cough, daytime sleepiness, fatigue, early morning headache.
  • Tell the team promptly, even if it seems mild.
  • The team checks breathing every 2 to 3 months and may order an overnight oxygen test or sleep study.

If you are a caregiver

  • Note when symptoms happen and pass it on.
  • Ask the team who to call out of hours.
When to get help Worsening breathlessness needs prompt review. If it is severe, call your local emergency number.
Sources: NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • Chest infections and mucus problems should be treated promptly. They are reversible causes of worse breathing.
  • A weak cough can be helped with breath stacking or manual assisted cough first, then a lung volume recruitment bag.
  • A mechanical cough assist device may be added, especially during an infection.

If you are a caregiver

  • Ask the physio or respiratory team to train you in assisted cough techniques now.
  • Keep their phone numbers where you can find them fast.
When to get help Fever, thick secretions or more breathlessness: contact the team or emergency services promptly.
Sources: NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • NIV can improve breathing symptoms and may prolong life, but it does not stop the disease from progressing.
  • It may be needed only at night at first, and for longer later.
  • It can be stopped at any time. Ask the team for help if it is uncomfortable.
  • Check that the mask system works with other equipment, such as eye-gaze devices.

If you are a caregiver

  • Ask for training and an assessment of your ability and willingness to help with NIV.
  • Raise concerns with the ventilation team.
When to get help Contact the ventilation team if the mask causes problems or the person is distressed. Medicines can ease breathlessness.
Sources: NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • Tell the team. Cramps and stiffness can be treated.
  • A physio can set an exercise programme that suits your strength and fatigue: active, assisted or passive.
  • Medicines exist, and the doctor chooses. NICE names quinine, then baclofen, among the options.

If you are a caregiver

  • Ask the physio to teach passive stretches and safe handling.
  • Check that you are able and willing to help with the programme.
When to get help New or severe pain should be reported to the team.
Sources: NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • Remove rugs and clutter, and keep walkways dry.
  • Use night lights or motion lights, and wear sturdy, well-fitting shoes.
  • For foot drop, ask about an ankle foot orthosis (AFO).
  • Do one thing at a time. Avoid talking while walking.
  • Rest when fatigued, take breaks and ask for help.

If you are a caregiver

  • Arrange a home assessment with an occupational therapist.
  • Keep paths clear, especially with children or pets around.
When to get help After a fall with injury, get medical help. Tell the team about any change in balance.
Sources: ALS Association: Fall prevention · NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • Ask the OT and physio early about equipment. NICE mentions riser-recliner chairs, hospital beds, commodes and hoists.
  • Equipment should be provided without delay and reviewed as needs change.

If you are a caregiver

  • Ask for training in safe manual handling before helping with transfers.
  • Do not lift alone without training.
When to get help A fall or near-fall during a transfer is a reason to ask for an earlier review.
Sources: NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • Install grab bars and use a shower seat or transfer tub bench.
  • Use a well-secured bath mat and pace yourself in the bathroom.

If you are a caregiver

  • Ask the OT for a bathroom assessment.
  • Keep floors dry and the way clear.
When to get help If washing feels unsafe, ask for an assessment now rather than after a fall.
Sources: ALS Association: Fall prevention · NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • Ask for an occupational therapy assessment of daily living, including personal care and work.
  • Ask about assistive technology and environmental control systems.
  • Use adaptive equipment, take breaks and ask for help when fatigued.

If you are a caregiver

  • Help where needed, but let them keep as much independence as they can.
When to get help Tell the team when tasks you did last month have become hard. Equipment should be provided without delay.
Sources: NICE NG42 · ALS Association: Fall prevention

If you live with ALS

  • Give speaking your full attention. Don't eat or drink while talking.
  • Speak slowly, pronounce each syllable, be brief and emphasise word endings.
  • Breathe every few words. Use gestures, spell out difficult words, or name the topic with one keyword.
  • Consider voice banking while your voice is still clear.

If you are a caregiver

  • Reduce background noise and look at the speaker.
  • Ask them to repeat or spell. Repeat back what you heard so they can fill in the gaps.
When to get help Ask for a speech and language therapy review at the first sign of change.
Sources: ALS Association: Speech and swallowing · NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • Low-tech tools such as alphabet or picture boards and high-tech tools such as tablets with speech output can both help.
  • Eye-tracking devices can let a person use the internet and type on custom screens.
  • Needs change, so ask for regular reviews. Complex eye-gaze equipment may need a specialist AAC service.

If you are a caregiver

  • Ask for training in how to use the equipment.
  • Give them time to answer and don't finish sentences.
When to get help Ask the team early. Equipment and training should arrive without delay.
Sources: NICE NG42 · NINDS: ALS · ALS Association: Speech and swallowing

If you live with ALS

  • Ask the team for a designated contact and for different ways to get in touch besides phone calls.
  • Tell the listener the topic first with a single keyword, and ask them to repeat back what they heard.
  • Keep a communication board and a medical information list in your go-bag.

If you are a caregiver

  • Be the translator for new people if you understand their speech well.
  • Carry the contact list and attend calls with them.
When to get help If you can't reach support, tell the team so alternative routes can be arranged.
Sources: NICE NG42 · ALS Association: Speech and swallowing · ALS Association: Emergency preparedness

  • Tell the team. Medicines can help with unwanted episodes of crying or laughing.

If you are a caregiver

  • Stay calm and wait for it to pass. It may not match their real feelings.
  • Explain it to friends and family.
When to get help If low mood or hopelessness seems to be behind the crying, tell the team.

If you live with ALS

  • Your team should ask about your emotional wellbeing. Tell them how you feel, including fears about dying, changes in relationships and intimacy.
  • Ask about support groups, online forums, counselling or psychology services.

If you are a caregiver

  • Your own emotional needs count and should be asked about too.
  • Join a caregiver support group.
When to get help If you have thoughts of harming yourself, contact your local emergency or crisis line right away.
Sources: NICE NG42

  • Topics include advance decisions to refuse treatment, do-not-resuscitate orders and a person to decide for you. Terms vary by country.
  • Make sure plans are written down and easy for professionals to find.

If you are a caregiver

  • Know their wishes and keep copies of documents.
  • Raise the topic earlier if speech or thinking may decline.
When to get help Ask for the conversation before a crisis, not during one.

If you live with ALS

  • Pack a go-bag: medical information list, insurance copies, advance directive information, ID, communication board, nutrition and breathing supplies, medications, glasses, cash and card, and a spare phone battery.
  • Plan for three cases: a hospital trip, leaving home, and a community evacuation.

If you are a caregiver

  • Know where the bag is and how to leave quickly.
  • Keep your team's out-of-hours number with the bag.
When to get help In an emergency, call your local emergency number. Ask the team about crisis plans ahead of time.
Sources: ALS Association: Emergency preparedness · NICE NG42

If you live with ALS

  • Burnout can come from perfectionism, never-ending tasks, work overload, impossible tasks, multiple roles, self-sacrifice or unspoken feelings.
  • Plan respite: trained in-home help, a volunteer, or a short break, discussed first with your loved one.
  • Ask the team about carer assessments, support groups and counselling.

If you are a caregiver

  • Take time for yourself, such as a walk, a hobby or time with friends.
  • Say how you feel out loud instead of holding it in.
